What is a calorie deficit?

A calorie deficit means eating less energy than your body uses. Your body makes up the difference from stored energy, mainly body fat. It is the basic principle behind every diet that works, whether it is called low-carb, intermittent fasting or clean eating.

How the calculator works

  1. We estimate your resting energy use (BMR) with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, one of the most accurate equations for healthy adults.
  2. We multiply it by an activity factor to estimate your maintenance calories (TDEE).
  3. We subtract 250, 500 or 750 kcal a day for a mild, moderate or faster plan, never going below 1,200 kcal for women or 1,500 kcal for men.
  4. We estimate weekly loss using roughly 7,700 kcal per kg of body fat. Real loss is usually a little slower than this simple rule, because your body adapts as you lose weight.

Which deficit should you choose?

  • Mild (−250 kcal): easiest to stick to, good if you are already fairly lean or want to build muscle at the same time.
  • Moderate (−500 kcal): the most common choice; steady loss without extreme hunger for most people.
  • Faster (−750 kcal): harder to sustain and more likely to cost muscle. Keep protein high and lift weights if you choose it.

A loss of about 0.5–1% of body weight a week is a sensible target for most people.

Make the deficit work

  • Keep protein high: about 1.6 g per kg of body weight helps protect muscle. Try our protein calculator.
  • Strength train 2–3 times a week so the weight you lose is mostly fat.
  • Walk more. Daily steps add up and are easy to recover from.
  • Track the trend. Weigh yourself a few mornings a week and compare weekly averages. If nothing changes in 2–3 weeks, reduce by another 100–200 kcal or add more steps.

Frequently asked questions

How big should my calorie deficit be?

For most people, 300 to 500 kcal a day below maintenance is a good start. It usually gives steady loss of about 0.25 to 0.5 kg a week without extreme hunger.

Is a 1,000 calorie deficit safe?

It is aggressive for many people and harder to sustain. It may be suitable for some people with a lot of weight to lose, ideally with medical advice. Smaller deficits are easier to keep.

Why am I not losing weight in a calorie deficit?

Usually the deficit is smaller than you think: portions, cooking oil, drinks and weekends add up. Water retention can also hide fat loss for a week or two. Track honestly and check weekly averages.

Do I need to count calories forever?

No. Many people count for a few weeks to learn portion sizes, then switch to simple habits such as the plate method.

Can I build muscle in a calorie deficit?

Beginners and people returning to training often can, with high protein and strength training. This is called body recomposition.
