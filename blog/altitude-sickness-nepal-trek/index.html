Altitude Sickness on Nepal Treks: Prevention, Symptoms and a Safe Pace

How to avoid altitude sickness on Everest, Annapurna and other Nepal treks: safe ascent rates, early warning signs, and when you must go down.

Altitude sickness is the most common reason treks in Nepal go wrong, and it has nothing to do with how fit you are. The best protection is going up slowly: above 3,000 m, sleep no more than about 500 m higher each night, take a rest day every three or four days, and never go higher while you have symptoms. That is the advice of the UK’s National Travel Health Network and Centre (TravelHealthPro), and it is what good trek itineraries are built around.

This guide explains what altitude sickness is, how to spot it early, and what to do. It is written from a trainer’s point of view, not a doctor’s: talk to a doctor or travel clinic before a high-altitude trek, especially about medicines.

What altitude sickness is

As you go higher, the air holds less oxygen with every breath. Your body can adjust, but it needs days, not hours. When you climb faster than your body can adjust, you can get ill. There are three forms:

Type What it is Main signs
Acute mountain sickness (AMS) The common, mild form Headache, plus nausea, dizziness, tiredness or weakness
High altitude cerebral oedema (HACE) Swelling of the brain; an emergency Confusion, unusual behaviour, poor coordination or stumbling
High altitude pulmonary oedema (HAPE) Fluid in the lungs; an emergency Breathless on effort, then even at rest, dry cough that becomes wet, chest tightness

TravelHealthPro notes that AMS symptoms usually start 6 to 10 hours after going above 2,500 m, while HAPE and HACE usually appear at least 2 days after going above 3,000 to 4,000 m. HAPE can happen without any AMS first.

Why fitness does not protect you

This surprises many people who train hard for their trek. “Physical fitness does not appear to protect against altitude illness,” says TravelHealthPro. The fitness you build still matters: it makes long trek days easier and safer. But a fit person who walks up too fast is at more risk than a slower person who follows the plan.

The best predictor is how you reacted at altitude before. If you got sick on a past trip, tell your doctor and choose a slower itinerary.

How to prevent it

  1. Follow a slow itinerary. Above 3,000 m, sleep no more than about 500 m higher each night, with a rest day at the same height every three or four days.
  2. Do not jump high in one day. Avoid going from below 1,200 m to above 3,500 m in a single day where you can.
  3. Climb high, sleep low. Many itineraries include a walk higher in the afternoon, then sleep lower. Your guide will plan this.
  4. Use the acclimatisation days. They are not wasted days. Take the short hike your guide suggests, eat well and rest.
  5. Walk slowly. Go at a pace where you can talk. On steep sections, slow down even more.
  6. Eat and drink normally. Keep eating even if you lose appetite. Dal bhat is easy to digest; see how much protein is in dal bhat.
  7. Ask a doctor about medicine before you go. Some trekkers are prescribed medicine to help prevent AMS. That is a decision for a doctor, not for a blog.

The rules if symptoms start

TravelHealthPro advises treating any new symptom at altitude as altitude illness until proven otherwise. In practice:

  • Mild headache or nausea (AMS): stop going up. Rest at the same height. Tell your guide.
  • Symptoms not better after one to two days, or getting worse: go down.
  • Confusion, stumbling, breathless at rest or a wet cough: this may be HACE or HAPE. Go down immediately and get medical help. Do not wait for the morning.
  • Never go higher to sleep while you still have symptoms.
  • Never leave someone with altitude illness alone, and never let them walk down alone.

Going down even a few hundred metres often makes a big difference. Helicopter rescue is common in Nepal, so check before you travel that your insurance covers rescue at the height you will trek to.

How this fits your training

Your training at home gets your legs and heart ready, and your trek pace protects you from altitude. You need both:

  • Before the trek: follow a plan such as our 8-week trek plan for flat countries or the Everest Base Camp training plan.
  • On the trek: walk slower than you think you need to, especially above 3,000 m, and respect the rest days.

For the Annapurna Circuit, the high point is Thorong La pass at about 5,416 m; read our Annapurna Circuit fitness guide for that route.

Frequently asked questions

At what height does altitude sickness start?

It can start above about 2,500 m. Most Everest and Annapurna treks spend many days above that height, so the slow-ascent rules matter on almost every popular trek in Nepal.

Can fit people get altitude sickness?

Yes. Fitness does not protect you. Fit trekkers are sometimes at more risk because they feel strong and walk up too fast.

How long does it take to acclimatise?

It depends on the person and the height. That is why good itineraries add height slowly over many days, with rest days every three or four days above 3,000 m.

Is it safe to keep going with a mild headache?

Do not go higher while you have symptoms. Rest at the same height and tell your guide. If it gets worse or does not improve over one to two days, go down.

Does drinking more water prevent altitude sickness?

Drinking normally and staying hydrated helps you feel better on long trek days, but water alone does not prevent altitude sickness. A slow ascent does.
