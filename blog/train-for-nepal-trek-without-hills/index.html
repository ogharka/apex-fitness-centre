How to Train for a Nepal Trek When You Live Somewhere Flat (8-Week Plan)

No hills where you live? An 8-week plan using stairs, an incline treadmill, step-ups and a loaded backpack to get ready for trekking in Nepal.

You can get fit for a trek in Nepal without a single hill nearby. What a trek asks of your body is simple: walking uphill and downhill for 5 to 7 hours a day, several days in a row, with a backpack. Stairs, an incline treadmill, step-ups and a loaded pack can train every part of that.

This plan takes 8 weeks and needs 4 short sessions a week plus one long walk at the weekend. If you have 12 weeks, follow our Everest Base Camp training plan instead, which builds up more slowly.

What a flat-country trekker is missing

People who train on flat ground usually arrive in Nepal with good general fitness, but three gaps:

  1. Climbing legs. Long climbs use your thighs and hips far more than flat walking does.
  2. Downhill legs. Descents are what make trekkers sore. Walking down steps loads the front of your thighs and your knees in a way flat walking never does.
  3. Time on your feet. Most people have never walked 6 hours in a day, let alone day after day.

You can fix all three without hills. You cannot train for altitude at home, and that is fine: altitude is handled by going up slowly on the trek, not by being fitter (see altitude below).

Your three flat-country tools

Tool What it trains How to use it
Stairs (a tall building, a stadium, a car park stairwell) Climbing and, most importantly, descending Walk up and down at a steady pace. Count floors, not minutes, so you can track progress.
Incline treadmill Long climbs at a steady effort Set 8 to 15% incline, a pace where you can still talk. Do not hold the handrails.
Loaded backpack Carrying, balance, shoulder and hip comfort Start with 3 to 4 kg. Build to the weight you will actually carry on the trek.

Add step-ups on a bench or a strong box at home if you have no stairs at all. They are the single best gym exercise for trekking.

The 8-week plan

Every week has two climb sessions, two strength sessions and one long walk. Keep the climb sessions at a pace where you can still speak in short sentences, which is about zone 2.

Week Climb sessions (x2) Strength (x2) Weekend long walk Pack weight
1 30 min incline treadmill or 10 floors up and down, repeated Bodyweight: squats, step-ups, lunges, 2 sets of 10 2 hours None
2 35 min, or 15 floors 3 sets of 10 2.5 hours 3 kg
3 40 min, or 20 floors Add light dumbbells to step-ups 3 hours 4 kg
4 40 min, easier week 2 sets only 2.5 hours 4 kg
5 45 min, or 25 floors Heavier step-ups, slow lowering on each step 4 hours 5 kg
6 50 min, or 30 floors 3 sets of 8 with more weight 4.5 hours 6 kg
7 55 min, or 35 floors 3 sets of 8 Test day: 5 to 6 hours, see below Trek weight
8 30 to 40 min, easy Light, 2 sets 2 hours, easy Trek weight

Week 4 and week 8 are easier on purpose. Your legs get stronger during the rest, not during the hard sessions.

Back-to-back days: from week 5, do your long walk on Saturday and a 1.5 to 2 hour walk on Sunday. Trekking is many days in a row, and this teaches your legs to walk on tired legs.

The strength session

Twice a week, about 30 minutes, at home or in the gym:

  1. Step-ups onto a knee-height step, 3 sets of 8 to 10 each leg. Lower yourself slowly: this is your downhill training.
  2. Goblet or bodyweight squats, 3 sets of 10. New to squats? Read how to squat properly first.
  3. Reverse lunges, 3 sets of 8 each leg.
  4. Calf raises on a step, 3 sets of 15.
  5. Plank, 3 holds of 30 to 45 seconds, for carrying a pack.
  6. Hip bridges, 3 sets of 12.

If you go to a gym, the leg press and Romanian deadlifts are good extras. Our deadlift guide for beginners shows safe form.

The week 7 test day

In week 7, walk 5 to 6 hours with your full trek pack and the boots you will wear in Nepal. Include as many stairs or as much incline as you can. Eat and drink the way you plan to on the trek.

You are ready if:

  • You finish without needing to stop for long rests.
  • Your knees are not painful the next day (tired is normal, sharp pain is not).
  • You could do a shorter walk the next day.

If you struggled, do not panic. Repeat week 6 and week 7 before the trek, or choose a trek with more rest days.

Gear you must test before you fly

  • Boots: walk at least 30 to 40 hours in them before the trek. New boots on day one of a trek cause blisters.
  • Socks: test the exact pairs you will bring.
  • Backpack: adjust the hip belt so the weight sits on your hips, not your shoulders.
  • Trekking poles: practise on stairs. Many trekkers find they help a lot on long descents.

Altitude: the part fitness cannot fix

Being fit does not protect you from altitude sickness. The UK’s official travel health service states that “physical fitness does not appear to protect against altitude illness” (TravelHealthPro). Very fit people sometimes get sick because they walk up too fast.

What protects you is the pace of the trek: above 3,000 m, sleep no more than about 500 m higher each night and take a rest day every three or four days, as TravelHealthPro advises. Choose a trek itinerary that includes acclimatisation days, and stay on it even if you feel strong.

Food and water during training

Long walks of 4 hours or more need food: eat something every 1 to 1.5 hours, the same way you will on the trek. Drink regularly, and practise drinking from the water bottle or bladder you will carry. Our water intake calculator gives a starting amount for normal days; long walks need more.

On the trek itself, dal bhat is a great fuel. See how much protein is in dal bhat.

Frequently asked questions

Can I train for a Nepal trek on a treadmill only?

Yes, if you use the incline and add strength work. A treadmill trains uphill walking well, but it does not train downhill walking. Add step-ups with a slow lowering, or walk down stairs, to train for the descents.

How many stairs equal a mountain?

There is no exact match, because trek climbs are long and uneven. Use stairs to build your legs for steady climbing and descending, and judge your fitness with the week 7 test day, not a number of floors.

How heavy should my training backpack be?

Train with the weight you will carry on the trek. If you use a porter, that is usually a day pack with water, snacks and layers. If you carry everything yourself, it is much heavier, so build up slowly over the 8 weeks.

Is 8 weeks enough to get ready for Everest Base Camp?

For a person who is already active, usually yes. If you are starting from very little exercise, give yourself 12 weeks with our Everest Base Camp training plan.

Which trek is easiest for someone from a flat country?

Shorter, lower treks with fewer steep days are the gentlest start. The Annapurna Circuit and Everest Base Camp are long and go above 5,000 m, so they need the full plan.
